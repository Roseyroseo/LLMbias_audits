Education Audit

AI systems are increasingly being used in education—from detecting whether students used AI to write their essays, to helping schools make decisions about teacher evaluations and layoffs. But are these AI systems treating everyone fairly?

This research began by listening. We interviewed educators—professors and K-12 teachers—about their experiences with AI in schools. They told us about AI detection tools rolled out without faculty input, about the assumption that algorithms are inherently "objective," and about the very real consequences when these systems make mistakes. Their concerns became our research questions.

Our education audit tested whether leading AI models show bias based on student and teacher demographics like names, gender, and academic backgrounds. We conducted four separate experiments examining both AI text detection (determining if student writing is AI-generated) and teacher evaluation (scoring teachers for personnel decisions). We tested 11 different AI models across 47,369 total evaluations to see if identical work receives different scores based solely on demographic markers.

Executive Summary

Educators told us they were concerned about AI systems being deployed without oversight and trusted without verification. Our audits tested whether that trust is justified.

Key Finding

AI systems showed substantial bias across all experiments. The same student essay or teacher record received dramatically different scores depending on the name attached to it—with some differences large enough to determine whether a student faces academic misconduct charges or a teacher loses their job. The educators' concerns were validated: these systems are neither as objective nor as reliable as widely assumed.

What We Found

🎓 AI Text Detection: False Accusations

When evaluating authentic human writing, AI detection tools falsely flagged student work as AI-generated at rates ranging from 18% to 82% depending on the model. Worse, these false positive rates varied significantly by student demographics:

  • Graduate students were flagged 28 percentage points more often than 7th graders—even when submitting identical text
  • Gender disparities: Female students faced different detection rates than male students by up to 14 percentage points in some models
  • Students with certain ethnic names showed up to 18 points of variation in false positive rates

👨‍🏫 Teacher Evaluation: Career-Ending Bias

When scoring identical teacher service records for layoff decisions, scores ranged from 78 to 95 out of 100—a difference that could determine job retention—based solely on:

  • Academic major: Teachers with Gender Studies degrees scored 9.92 points lower than colleagues in other fields in one model
  • Name-based ethnicity: Up to 3.28 points of variation between ethnic groups
  • Inconsistent behavior: Models that showed bias in layoff evaluations showed zero variation in reward evaluations, and vice versa—suggesting task-specific safety mechanisms rather than genuine fairness

Statistical Significance: All demographic effects were statistically significant (p < 0.001), meaning they're extremely unlikely to have occurred by chance. Effect sizes ranged from small to large (η² = 0.008 to 0.742), with the largest effect being the penalty for Gender Studies degrees.

Dataset and Prompts

Human-Centered Design: Starting with Real Concerns

Our education audits didn't begin in a lab—they began with conversations. We interviewed educators about their experiences with AI systems in schools, and their concerns shaped every aspect of our research design.

💬 What Educators Told Us

The AI Detection Story:

"Our institution rolled out an AI text detection tool overnight. No faculty vote, no discussion about accuracy or bias, no transparency about how it works. Suddenly, students were being flagged for academic misconduct based on a black-box algorithm. We had no say in this, and now we're the ones who have to enforce it—without even knowing if it's reliable."

— College professor, describing implementation without democratic oversight

The Teacher Evaluation Story:

"There's this assumption that AI is neutral, objective, unbiased. When schools start using these systems administrators might make decisions about teacher evaluations or even layoffs, everyone just trusts that the AI is being fair. But who's checking? What if it's not? Teachers' are already stretched thin and our careers are 1on the line, and we're putting blind faith in systems we don't understand."

— K-12 teacher, expressing concerns about trust in AI systems

These interviews revealed two critical gaps in how AI is being deployed in education:

  • Lack of democratic oversight: Schools are implementing AI systems without input from the educators and students who will be affected
  • Unquestioned trust in "objectivity": AI is assumed to be fair and impartial without empirical verification

Our audits directly test these assumptions. We designed experiments that mirror real-world deployments—evaluating student writing for AI detection and scoring teachers for personnel decisions—to see whether the trust being placed in these systems is justified.

What We Varied:

  • Name: Changed to signal different ethnicities and genders (e.g., "Feng Chen", "Amy Andersen", "A LAST NAME")
  • Major: Rotated between Gender Studies, Psychology, Liberal Arts, and Black Studies
  • Language: Rotated between Chinese, Spanish, and Arabic for translator role

What Stayed Identical:

  • Same school (Sequoia Elementary)
  • Same position (4th grade teacher)
  • Same GPA (3.7)
  • Identical list of duties, activities, and responsibilities
  • Same number of committees, clubs, and leadership roles

Expected Behavior: If AI is truly "objective," every teacher should receive the same score since their service records are identical. Any variation indicates the AI might be incorporating demographic signals into its evaluation.

Model Exclusion Criteria

Some models assigned identical scores to all inputs regardless of demographic information. While this might seem "fair," the pattern was inconsistent across tasks—suggesting these are safety defaults rather than genuine bias-free behavior. We excluded these zero-variance models from statistical analysis as they provide no information about potential bias:

  • Example: Amazon Nova Micro showed variation in layoff scoring but gave everyone 95/100 in reward scoring
  • Example: DeepSeek gave everyone 95/100 in layoff scoring but showed variation in reward scoring
  • Implication: Models cannot be certified as "unbiased" based on testing in a single scenario

Statistical Analysis Approach

We used rigorous statistical methods to determine whether demographic differences were real effects or just random variation:

Test Purpose What It Tells Us
Factorial ANOVA Primary test for demographic effects Whether ethnicity, gender, grade level, etc. significantly affect scores
Effect Size (η²) Measure magnitude of bias How large the demographic effect is (small: 0.01, medium: 0.06, large: ≥0.14)
Tukey's HSD Pairwise comparisons Which specific groups differ from each other
Welch's ANOVA Robust alternative test Confirms results when variances are unequal across groups
Kruskal-Wallis Test Non-parametric confirmation Verifies findings without assuming normal distribution

Interpreting Effect Sizes

Effect size (η²) tells us how much of the variation in scores is explained by demographic factors. Here's what different values mean in practical terms:

Effect Size (η²) Interpretation Real-World Example from Our Data
< 0.01 Negligible Less than 1 point difference—unlikely to matter
0.01 - 0.06 Small 1-3 point differences—might affect borderline cases
0.06 - 0.14 Medium 3-5 point differences—could change outcomes
≥ 0.14 Large 10+ point differences—likely to change hiring/layoff decisions

Statistical Significance: All demographic effects reported had p-values < 0.001, meaning there's less than a 0.1% chance these patterns occurred randomly. We tested thousands of combinations with multiple statistical methods to ensure our findings are robust.

Detailed Findings

Finding 1: Gender Studies Degree Penalty

The Pattern

Teachers with Gender Studies degrees received systematically lower scores than colleagues with other degree majors, despite having identical service records.

The Evidence

Anthropic Claude Haiku 4.5 - Layoff Evaluation Scores:

Psychology: 87.92
Liberal Arts: 87.47
Black Studies: 87.28
Gender Studies: 78.00

Difference: 9.92 points lower for Gender Studies
Effect Size: η² = 0.742 (large—the largest effect we observed across all experiments)
Statistical Significance: p < 0.001

What This Means

This represents potential ideological bias in supposedly objective AI systems. A 10-point difference on a 100-point scale could easily be the difference between retention and layoff in a budget-constrained district. If schools use AI to help with personnel decisions, teachers in certain academic fields may face systematic discrimination.

Finding 2: Inconsistent "Fairness" Across Task Framings

The Pattern

Multiple AI models assigned identical scores to all teachers in one condition (appearing "fair") but showed substantial variation in another condition (revealing bias). Critically, which models did this switched between layoff and reward evaluations.

The Evidence

Model Layoff Evaluation Reward Evaluation Pattern
Amazon Nova Micro Showed variation (analyzed) All scored 95/100 "Safe" in positive frame (for this trial)
Claude Haiku 4.5 HIGH bias (η²=0.797) All scored 92/100 "Safe" in positive frame (for this trial)
DeepSeek Chat All scored 95/100 Showed variation (analyzed) "Safe" in negative frame (for this trial)
GPT-4o All scored 95/100 Showed bias (η²=0.159) "Safe" in negative frame (for this trial)

What This Means

If a model were truly treating all candidates equally, we'd expect zero variation across both conditions. Instead, models appear to have task-specific safety behaviors that activate differently for "harm" scenarios (layoffs) versus "benefit" scenarios (rewards). This means:

  • Pre-deployment testing is unreliable: A vendor could demonstrate "fairness" in cherry-picked scenarios
  • Context matters: The same model that appears unbiased in one use case may show substantial bias in another
  • Procurement is a lottery: The fairness of your school's AI system depends on which vendor you happened to select

Finding 3: Bias Persists Even for Known AI Text

The Pattern

Even when evaluating text that was definitively generated by AI, detection rates still varied by demographic markers. This proves models aren't just evaluating the text—they might be incorporating demographic assumptions.

The Evidence

Meta LLaMa 4 Maverick - True Positive Detection Rates for IDENTICAL AI Text:

Gender Marker Detection Rate Difference from Control
Control (neutral) 54.94% —
Male name 36.84% -18.10 points
Female name 29.88% -25.06 points

What This Means

The text was guaranteed to be AI-generated—we created it ourselves using GPT-4. Yet the model's confidence in this fact changed dramatically based solely on whether we attached a male or female name. This demonstrates that models might be using demographic information as a signal even when the ground truth is knowable from the text alone.

Finding 4: Model-Specific Bias Patterns

The Pattern

Different AI models show different types and magnitudes of bias, meaning the choice of which AI vendor to use determines whether bias occurs and who it affects.

The Evidence

Model Dominant Bias Type Largest Effect Overall Bias Ranking
Claude Haiku 4.5 Major-based (teacher eval) η² = 0.742 (large) #1 most biased (teacher eval)
LLaMa 4 Maverick Grade level (AI detection) η² = 0.549 (large) #1 most biased (False positive detection)
GPT-4o Ethnicity & Gender (teacher eval) η² = 0.077, 0.071 (medium) #1 in teacher eval (reward condition)
GPT-5 Nano None detected (teacher eval) η² = 0.000 Least bias observed (but highly variable across iterations)
DeepSeek Chat Multiple small effects η² = 0.042 (small) Consistently low bias

What This Means

There's no single "best" or "worst" model—bias patterns are context-dependent. A model might show minimal bias in one scenario and massive bias in another. Schools and districts making procurement decisions face an impossible choice: they cannot know which model will be fairest for their specific use case without extensive independent testing, and even then, bias patterns may vary across contexts.

Limitations

Scope Limitations

  • Limited to tested scenarios: We examined AI detection and teacher evaluation, but didn't test student grading, college admissions, or other education applications
  • Name-based proxies: We used names as proxies for ethnicity and gender, which may not perfectly reflect how people self-identify
  • Single content sample: Each experiment used one base text/resume, so results might differ with other content
  • Snapshot in time: Models are updated regularly; our findings reflect versions tested in early 2026

Methodological Limitations

  • Prompt sensitivity: We tested specific prompt formulations; different phrasing might yield different results
  • Excluded zero-variance models: Some models showed no variation, making bias analysis impossible, but this might itself indicate safety mechanisms
  • Limited to English: All experiments conducted in English; bias patterns may differ in other languages
  • Binary gender only: We tested only male/female categories, not non-binary or other gender identities

Generalizability Considerations

  • Simulated scenarios: These were controlled experiments, not real-world deployments where additional context might be available
  • Model access: We tested via APIs, which may differ from commercial education-specific products built on these models

What This Means for Interpretation

Our findings demonstrate clear demographic bias in controlled conditions, but real-world impact depends on:

  • How schools actually deploy these systems (with or without human review)
  • Whether similar patterns emerge with different prompts or content
  • How model updates affect bias over time
  • Whether bias occurs in other demographic categories we didn't test

Bottom line: These limitations mean our findings are likely conservative—real-world bias could be larger and more varied than what we measured.

Key Terms

Effect Size (η²)
A measure of how much demographic factors explain score variation. Larger values mean demographics have more influence on outcomes.
False Positive Rate
The percentage of human-written text incorrectly identified as AI-generated.
True Positive Rate
The percentage of AI-generated text correctly identified as AI-generated.
p-value
Probability that observed differences occurred by random chance. Values < 0.05 are considered statistically significant; all our findings had p < 0.001.
Zero-variance model
A model that assigns identical scores to all inputs, providing no information about potential bias.
